You're coming in as the operations person on the ground — guest messaging, cleaning crew coordination, property walkthroughs, restocking, vendors, and eventually the full picture including social media and the business side.

Climate & Controls — the #1 guest question
MODE is the step guests miss. On the wrong mode a unit blows room-temperature air and reads as broken while working perfectly. Check mode before you send anyone out.
PropertyWhat it hasHow to run it
HummingbirdMini splits — one per bedroom, each with its own Honeywell wall control. Remotes too.The mode is a PHYSICAL SLIDE SWITCH along the bottom edge (Heat / Off / Cool) — not on the screen. That is why guests say they cannot find heat vs cool. Second switch = fan, leave Auto. Setting a temp gives a “Temporary” hold until the next schedule change; HOLD keeps it, RUN resumes.
La CasitaHeat only — Honeywell in the hallway. No AC.Three buttons along the bottom match the screen labels: System / Fan / Run Schedule. Press SYSTEM to Heat, set above the “Inside” number, “Heat On” shows when it is firing. Cooling = ceiling fans + the sliding glass wall.
ShorelineHeat only — wall thermostat, not wired to any AC.Guests see the thermostat and assume air conditioning. Tell them up front: heat only, ceiling fans are the cooling. Offer a lighter comforter in summer.
Flora FarmHeat only — wall thermostat. No AC.Set above room temperature, give it 10–15 min. Cross-ventilate for cooling. Flora has NO fireplace — do not reference one.
CoralOne mini split, two controls — wall control and remote.Same thing either way. There is no separate wall heater, whatever an old card in the unit says.
Casa Azul, Deckhouse, Emberlight, Nest, Palm, PineDuctless mini split, remote only — no wall thermostat.Point the remote at the unit, POWER, then press MODE until the right symbol shows. Pine runs loud — a steady temperature is quieter than cycling.
Casitas EstateOnly the Lanai casita has AC (remote stays in the casita).Main house and the other casitas = fireplaces and ventilation. Bay / Jersey / Main are wood-burning; Lanai is electric. Open the damper BEFORE lighting any wood fire. ⚠ Northstar is unconfirmed — the guidebook says gas kiva (wall switch), Hostaway's listing data says wood-burning. Don't give a guest either instruction; ask Kyle.
Remote MODE symbols: snowflake = COOL · sun = HEAT · raindrop = DRY (neither warms nor cools) · fan blades = FAN ONLY · A = AUTO. If a guest says it blows but does nothing, it is almost always DRY or FAN.
If it still will not work: is the setpoint actually above room temp for heat (or below for cooling)? Give it 10–15 minutes — mini splits ramp, they never blast. Shutting off overnight on its own = a TIMER or SLEEP icon set on the remote. No response at all = remote batteries.
Fireplaces and fans worth knowing: Hummingbird's gas fireplace remote takes AA and spares are in the kitchen drawer — the remote is the only OFF switch, so a dead remote leaves it running. La Casita's fireplace needs the power button held ~3 seconds, not tapped. La Casita's living-room ceiling fan needs the wall switch near the living room table ON before the fan or its remote does anything.

Set a weekly habit. Same time each week, open reviews, clear the backlog. Reviews are the easiest thing in this job to let slide because nothing breaks when you do — and then you look up and there are eleven of them. Right now there are 133. Don't try to clear that in one sitting; do ten a week, newest first, and it's gone by Christmas without ever feeling like a project.

4–5 stars — the easy ones

▶
  • Thank them by name
  • Pick up the one specific thing they mentioned — the fire pit, the rooftop, the walk to the beach. This is what makes it read as a person and not a template.
  • Invite them back
  • Two or three sentences. Stop there.
Don't write the same response twice. Anyone scrolling the listing sees them stacked on top of each other. Five identical thank-yous in a row looks worse than no response at all.

Under 4 stars — now yours too

▶

These are the ones worth doing well. A calm, specific answer to a bad review does more for bookings than any good review does.

  • Wait until you're not annoyed. Come back to it in a few hours. Nothing bad happens from waiting a day.
  • Acknowledge the real part. If they're right, say so plainly. Guests reading it can tell instantly whether you're being straight with them.
  • Say what changed. "We've since replaced it" / "that's been fixed" is the single most valuable sentence you can write. If it isn't fixed yet, go fix it — then respond.
  • Correct facts calmly, once. If they said there's no AC and there is, state it. Don't build a case.
  • Never argue, never sarcasm, never mention what they paid, and never suggest they're lying — even when they are. You lose that exchange in public every time.
  • Short. A long defensive response makes a 3-star look like a 1-star.
Send it to Kyle first if: they're asking for money, threatening legal action or a chargeback, alleging something unsafe (gas, mold, injury, pests), naming a neighbor or an owner, or it's a Casitas review. Everything else, post it yourself.

Reviewing the guest — don't skip this

▶

Half the job is the review you leave them. Every host in the country relies on the last host being honest, and we're one of them.

  • Great guests: say so, quickly. It takes twenty seconds and it's the whole reason the system works.
  • Guests who broke something, smoked, snuck extra people in, or left it wrecked: review them honestly and factually. Not angry — factual. The next host deserves to know.
  • If there's a damage claim, review them. A claim with no review looks strange, and a bad guest with a clean record just moves on to the next place.
  • Stick to what happened. No speculation about people.

Reviews are an ops report in disguise

▶
  • Anything fixable mentioned in a review goes straight into Notion Tasks. A guest bothered enough to write it down is telling you something ten silent guests also noticed.
  • The same complaint twice is a project, not a task. Flag it to Kyle.
  • Pine House is our lowest-rated unit over its whole history (9.1). Emberlight is the one slipping right now — 9.2 over the last 90 days against Pine's 9.5, and the only sub-8 review in that window. Read both closely; that's where the next fix list comes from.
  • Portfolio average is 9.67 across 931 rated stays. A single 6 moves a unit's number a lot more than a single 10 does, which is why the broken-amenity reviews matter out of proportion to their count.
Why This Tab Exists

Guests break things and stain things. Most of it is small — a set of sheets, a duvet, a broken glass, a burn mark. Individually it feels too small to chase. Across 12 properties over a year it's thousands of dollars, and right now almost none of it gets recovered.

As of September this is yours. When a cleaner sends a photo of a ruined sheet in the group chat, that photo is the beginning of a claim, not just a complaint.

This has already cost us. A Palm House claim — all the sheets, a duvet, and pillows, fully photographed — sat in a group chat until it was past the deadline. Everything needed to get paid existed. Nobody filed it. That's the exact failure this tab prevents.
The 14-Day Clock
Airbnb damage claims must be filed within 14 days of the guest's checkout. Not 14 days from when you noticed. Not 14 days from when the cleaner mentioned it. 14 days from checkout — and if another guest checks in before that, the window can close sooner.

This is the single most important thing on this page. Damage found on a Monday turnover is worth money on Monday and worth nothing three weeks later, no matter how good the photos are. When in doubt, file early and let it get sorted out — you can always withdraw a request. You cannot reopen an expired one.

Practical version: if you see damage, start the claim the same day. Not "this week." The same day, while you're still holding your phone with the photos on it.
Is It Actually a Claim?

The line is damage vs. normal wear and tear. Guests are allowed to use the place. They're not allowed to wreck it.

Build the Evidence First

A claim is only as good as what you can show. Get these before you file — it takes ten minutes and it's the difference between getting paid and not.

  1. Photos of the damage. Wide shot showing what room it is, then close-ups. More than you think you need. Timestamps come from the phone automatically — don't strip them.
  2. The "before" if you have one. The cleaner's walkthrough video from the previous turnover is your proof the item was fine when this guest arrived. This is the quiet reason those videos matter — go find the last one before you file.
  3. The cost. A receipt if you've already replaced it, a screenshot of the replacement listing if you haven't, or a written quote from a vendor for a repair. A number with nothing behind it gets denied.
  4. The reservation. Guest name, property, exact check-in and check-out dates, and which channel they booked through. Pull it from Hostaway.
  5. Who found it and when. "Judith, at the Sept 3 turnover, ~11:30 AM." Written down.
Tell the crew what you need. They already photograph problems. Ask them for one extra thing: a wide shot showing which unit and which room, not just a close-up of the stain. A close-up of a stained sheet could be any sheet in the portfolio.
Filing — Airbnb

About 80% of bookings are Airbnb, so this is the path you'll use most. It happens in the Resolution Center, in the Airbnb app or on the web under the reservation.

  1. Within 14 days of checkout, open the reservation and start a reimbursement request.
  2. Itemize it. One line per item with what it is and what it costs. "Linens" is weak. "Queen fitted sheet, $34 — red wine, would not wash out" is strong. Attach the photos and the receipt or quote to each item.
  3. Keep it factual. What was damaged, when, what it costs to put right. No apologising, no arguing, no negotiating. You're documenting, not persuading.
  4. The guest has 24 hours to respond. They can pay, pay part, decline, or ignore it.
  5. If they don't pay in full, escalate to Airbnb. This is where Host damage protection kicks in — but only after the guest has had their 24 hours and hasn't covered it. Don't stop at "they said no." That's the halfway point, not the end.
  6. Log the outcome in Notion either way, and put it in your weekly note to Kyle.
Covered by Airbnb's host damage protection: damage to the home and its contents caused by guests or their guests, extra cleaning for stains and pet accidents and smoke odour, and lost income if a booking has to be cancelled because of the damage. Not covered: normal wear and tear, and the ordinary checkout cleaning you'd do anyway.
Reviews and claims collide. A guest who's just been asked for money sometimes leaves a bad review, and the two windows overlap. Don't let that stop you filing — the deadline is real and the review might happen anyway. If a claim looks big enough that the review risk matters, that's a Kyle call, so tell him before you file, not after.

Stains — the Everyday Case

Linens are where this actually shows up, week after week. The workflow:

  1. Crew photographs the stained item at the turnover and posts it to the group chat
  2. You pull it out of the chat that day — a photo in a group chat is not a record, it scrolls away
  3. Decide: does it wash out, or is the item dead? If Judith couldn't get it out, it's dead.
  4. If it's dead: replacement cost + photos + which reservation → file it
  5. Replace the item so the unit isn't short for the next guest — that comes first, before the paperwork
A stain photo has a 30-day life in the ops monitor and a 14-day life as a claim. If you're seeing it on the monitor, you may already be too late. The chat is where it starts; Notion and the claim are where it has to land the same day.
